# Suggested Journals

These are proposed longform song profiles that keep the most revealing and personal material from the original and edited Instagram drafts while stripping out the repeated launch copy.

## Joint Queen

The process of making this record started quietly. Walls and I had been playing music together for almost two years, bringing our own songs to the project, supporting each other's work, and building a real friendship. But when I look back on that period, the dominant feeling is that we were not at the heart of it yet. The songs were not the problem. The problem was that the music had not fully become Us.

Then John played me the tracks he had been building. I had the same jolt I felt when I first heard Public Enemy as a teenager: groove, raw emotion, creative synthesis, a sound that felt both external and uncannily personal. As soon as I recorded a note of guitar, I knew I had stepped into a journey that had no analogue in my life. I was working with a deeply gifted musician and engineer who was also becoming my best friend. In ways I am still trying to understand, the work was saving my life.

Joint Queen felt like the correct beginning because it carried joy instead of explanation. It sounded open. It sounded alive. It sounded like a record finally trusting itself. For me, that song is still tied to the moment music stopped being a thing I longed for from the outside and became a life I could actually inhabit.

— Terry Devine

## Stash Daddy

Stash Daddy begins with something small and almost absurd: a kitchen-table conversation, a hand-drawn logo, a title that landed before I really understood what kind of freedom the song would demand. At that point I still thought recording was supposed to be ritualized, tense, expensive, and overprepared. Then John would set the track looping in my headphones, leave the room, and tell me to record some guitars.

That simple gesture changed me. The first few times it happened I froze, because my whole body was trained to think there had to be a correct answer before I played anything. But there was no rehearsing here. There was only creating. Standing alone with the track thumping in my headphones, I started to feel what it meant to speak in real time with no filter: through guitar, through voice, through instinct.

When John wrote and sang, "I feel too much. It's not enough," the line landed in me with unusual force. Stash Daddy became the document of that moment, the sound of my heart being set free inside a process that finally made room for it.

— Terry Devine

## Space Cruiser

Space Cruiser was born the morning after one of the worst shows of my life. I woke up carrying the kind of doubt that makes you question not just a performance but the entire enterprise of trying to make music in the first place. The night before, a friend had shown me a documentary about Icelandic music, and what stayed with me was the idea that the only sane way to continue was to make the music you genuinely wanted to hear.

So I wrote something to clear the taste out of my mouth. At the time I did not know it was part of an album. I did not even know it would survive. Then months later, once Joint Queen and Stash Daddy existed, the song reappeared as the missing third door in the Bong Tour trilogy. That was also when the larger method of Volume 1 started to reveal itself: collage editing, delays, stitched atmospheres, and arrangements discovered through listening rather than imposed beforehand.

The final version carries that whole path in it. Ishan's sargam part widened the horizon of the track. Terry's live bong rip completed it in the most unserious and somehow correct way possible. Space Cruiser became our proof that a song could begin in doubt and still arrive as myth.

— John Walls

## Home

Home is inseparable from the period after I returned from Los Angeles, where I had been moving through addiction and self-destruction at a speed that felt unsustainable. I did not really want to leave, but some quieter instinct was asking for another life. When I got back to Salt Lake, the house was almost empty. I slept on the floor that first night and felt my nervous system reset in a way I did not think was possible.

That experience became the emotional center of the song. Home did not ask me to explain myself. It did not care what I had done or what I thought I had ruined. It simply received me. Later, an old one-take guitar performance from a former bandmate became the foundation of the track. I kept returning to it across years, until it finally gathered the parts that let it become itself. By the end, family had entered the record too, and the song had shifted from a document of collapse into a document of return.

What I love about Home is that it does not pretend recovery is neat. It only insists that peace can still be found, and that sometimes the place you return to can teach you how to carry that peace forward.

— John Walls

## Decay

Decay marks the moment I finally understood the depth of trust inside this project. John made some small joking remark about it being our metal song, and I felt an entire internal permission structure rearrange itself. Until then, I do not think I fully understood that he wanted my actual instincts, not a version of myself trimmed down to fit someone else's expectations.

That realization mattered because the guitars on Decay came from a deeper place than craft alone. They came from hours of wailing, searching, letting something feral and emotional speak without apology. When I heard the spoken line, "Death was contained in me / Expressed as life," it felt less like somebody else's lyric than a truth I had been carrying without language.

Decay is personal to me because it captures the moment intuition became sacred. The song does not just sound heavy. It sounds like collapse being transformed into a live current, like the violence of experience refusing to stay buried.

— Terry Devine

## Resolve

Resolve comes from the part of life where everything narrows to a single decision. There are eras when you are forced to choose whether you will rise above what is destroying you or let it finish the job. For me, that is the territory the song inhabits. It is about the crucible, and about the discipline required to become someone reliable to yourself.

What I wanted the lyric to hold was not generic inspiration but the lived weight of self-recognition. The soul and the body coming into congruence. Intention becoming action. The old self not being erased, but burned down into a form that can no longer rule the terms. Some selves are meant to become ash so something truer can rise.

Resolve is the song of that turning point. It does not describe the whole journey, but it captures the instant where the internal contract becomes binding.

— John Walls

## Poetry

Poetry began in heartbreak and immediately complicated its own existence by carrying a title that felt almost impossible to earn. I did not want to write a bad poem, a corny poem, or a song that sounded like a performance of literary seriousness. The only line that gave me a way forward was Marianne Moore's insistence that there is, after all, a place for the genuine.

That became the central task of the song: find the genuine thing underneath the performance of music, underneath heartbreak, underneath vanity, fragility, fantasy, and self-protection. I wrote until my arm hurt. The body was in pain, but the soul was trying to break free. Then Terry entered the room and expanded the song beyond anything I had initially imagined. Some of the lines that now define it came from him, which feels right because the final piece is not about singular authorship so much as shared humanity.

Poetry matters to me because it does not arrive clean. It arrives alive. It lets the mess stay visible long enough for the life inside it to fight its way through.

— John Walls

## Gratitude

Gratitude meant something to me from the start, but it changed form once it took its place as the closing track. When John showed me the sequence and this song held the final spot, I felt something lock into place emotionally. My relationship to the track deepened through my voice, my guitar, and the sense that I was hearing not just a song but a destination.

There is a very specific feeling inside Gratitude for me: the recognition that something I had always wanted from life was no longer imaginary. Not a dashed dream, not a projection, not a fantasy I could romanticize from afar. Something real, happening now, through work and trust and brotherhood. I have cried listening to this song. I have also felt it give me a steadier inner ground.

That is why it belongs at the end of the record. Gratitude is not just a soft ending. It is the sound of peace arriving after a long season of pressure.

— Terry Devine
